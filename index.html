When is there snow?

Possible avalanche activity

Sentinel-1 radar can show where the snow surface changed between two passes 12 days apart. We call these areas snow-change patches. They indicate possible avalanche activity; the patches are unconfirmed. Two cases were examined.

Mahmud Dere, Dec 2017

Local report of an avalanche, 24–25 Dec 2017.

Patches within 1.5 km of the reported point: 3 on orbit 101 (19→31 Dec) and 5 on orbit 6 (13→25 Dec). Two patches overlap exactly between the two orbits, 425 m and 762 m from the reported point, at about 2,900–3,000 m. Timing: between 19 Dec and the early morning of 25 Dec.

Orbit 6, 25 Dec→6 Jan: none. Quiet control: 4 patches, none on the same spots. But 36 of 161 December patches box-wide are seen by both orbits, so the storm changed the snow widely.

April 2025

Storm 15–16 Apr, warm spell 21–27 Apr.

202 candidate patches (orbit 101, 17→29 Apr). Without a snow mask the first version gave 1,416, mostly snowmelt at low elevation.

16 also found by orbit 6, 186 not. That 8% agreement is about what chance gives, so it is not independent confirmation. None of the 202 overlap the quiet control (25 patches) or the summer control (158). The 26 strongest were checked in Sentinel-2 (20 m): no identifiable debris.

Next step: storm-triggered monitoring

Radar can only map possible avalanche activity after the fact, so the question is when to look. We propose a new radar check whenever the weather data show a trigger: 15 mm or more of snowfall (water) over 3 days, a sudden warm spell after snow, or heavy rain on snow. We tested these rules on the last 10 winters. They catch both known cases, Mahmud Dere in December 2017 and the April 2025 storm, and pick the same radar pairs we ran. Over a winter this means about 6 radar checks per orbit, because several events fall into the same 12-day pair. The first thing to run is a pair never tested: orbit 101, 5→17 April 2025, straight after the storm. The thresholds are starting values to recalibrate with real avalanche records. This builds a record of past events; it is not a warning system.

What the evidence suggests

What the evidence shows

About a third of the planning area (33%) is on slopes of 30° or more. The three Qızılqaya lines spend 0–4% of their length on such slopes. Snow lies from about 1 Nov to 19 Apr at 2,500–2,750 m and from 7 Oct to 25 May at 3,000–3,250 m. Radar suggests probable avalanche activity at Mahmud Dere in December 2017; the April 2025 patches are unconfirmed.

What it suggests for discussion

A small pilot on routes that avoid steep slopes, in the early season (Nov–Dec) and in spring (Mar–May), is worth discussing. These are candidates for professional assessment, not approved routes. Storm-triggered radar checks could build a local avalanche record.

What needs the park's decision first

Field assessment by certified avalanche professionals; a snow station and bulletin with Hydromet; rescue cover with the Emergencies Ministry; permits and border-zone rules with the State Border Service; a wildlife assessment.

Limits

Planning evidence, not a safety assessment. None of these layers says a slope is safe on any given day. Avalanche danger depends on the current snowpack and weather, and Shahdag has no avalanche forecast yet.
  • Sentinel-1A only; each orbit repeats every 12 days, so event dates are known only to a 12-day window.
  • Only orbits 101 (ascending) and 6 (descending) cover the study box; orbits 28 and 79 just graze it.
  • The radar shadow/layover mask is simple (it calls about 98% of the box visible).
  • The 30 m slope map misses short steep rolls and cornices; the "below steep slopes" runout measure is crude.
  • MODIS 500 m describes elevation bands, not individual slopes; snow cover is not snow depth.
  • ERA5-Land cells are about 11 km and run warm on peaks.
  • The December 2017 test rests on one local report; the point may mark the valley rather than the deposit.
  • Monitoring triggers use daily ERA5-Land box averages: they underestimate snow on the peaks, and a daily maximum temperature says nothing about the hour the satellite passes. Thresholds are starting values to recalibrate with real avalanche records.
  • The monthly snow maps give each elevation band and slope direction one average value, so they show general patterns, not snow at a particular spot.
